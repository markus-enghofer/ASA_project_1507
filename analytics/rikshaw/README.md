## Rikshaw

### Data Collection

Data was collected using the AgileX LIMO PRO robot mounted on an auto-rikshaw while driving within Mysuru for around 5 minutes. The position of the robot was not fixed rigidly, but it was placed in its box, such that movement of the robot relative to the vehicle should have been minimal. Moreover, the orientation of the robot with respect to which the gyroscope data is provided adheres to the orientation of the auto-rikshaw. Hence, no coordinate transformation was required. The data is divided into three frames. Each of them covers one to three minutes of the ride. Labels for potholes and speedbreakers were recorded manually.

### Preprocessing

As the robot was not moving itself and does not have any GPS sensors, no information about the speed of the vehicle is available. The only data sources are hence the accelerometer and gyroscope. Analogously to the handling of the agilex dataset, both covariates were smoothed by replacing the raw values by the weighted mean over a sequence of consecutive observations with respect to the normal distribtion. Since one of the main characteristica of road anomalies are the large ranges of both linear acceleration and angular velocity, only the positive or negative part of the respective values, whichever was larger, were considered. For each parameter, the standard deviation of the normal distribution and the number of considered observations for the smoothing were chosen such that they maximize the ratio of the sum of the ranges within a 2-second-frame around the labeled road anomalies and the standard deviation of the entire frame. This was done using the function find_optimal_parameters().

### Results

It turned out that even the raw data about the linear acceleration in z-direction as well as angular velocity around x-axis and y-axis are already quite good predictors for road anomalies. The ranges of linear acceleration in z-direction as well as angular velocity around both x- and y-axis are usually much higher in a 2-second-neighborhood around recorded speebreakers and especially potholes than during other time frames (see excel-table). However, even better results were achieved for all three covariates by taking the weighted mean over five consecutive values with standard deviation 2. Additionally, vertical speed, pitch and roll were calculated from the available data by accumulation of linear acceleration and angular velocity, respectively, but the data seems to be too noisy to avoid drift.
Based on the available data, it seems to be manageable to distinguish potholes and speedbreakers. The training data suggests that for speedbreakers, the positive and negative peaks of the angular-velocity around x-axis had approximately the same absolute value, whereas the negative peaks are significantly higher for potholes. Furthermore, it is expected that speedbreakers cause a positive peak, when the vehicle s upwards, closely followed by a negative peak, when it moves downwards again, whereas it is the other way round for potholes. This could not have been confirmed empirically based on the available data so far, but can be examined in future.
